## The phone never rang

My mother's phone sat on the table all morning, silent. No ring, no threatening call, no unknown number pressing a button. She was calm, because the phone looked fine and the line worked: she called people, and people answered.

And still, every call that came to her number was going somewhere else. She just never heard it. This isn't one person's story — it's a method, and the method repeats in exactly the same shape.

What makes it dangerous isn't that it's violent. It's that it's quiet. Everything we tell each other about fraud assumes the victim will notice something: someone will say something odd, ask for something, put them under pressure. This asks for nothing, so it leaves no feeling behind. Which means the check has to be done by someone else — someone holding the phone in their hand.

## What it is: call forwarding

Call forwarding is not new and not hidden. It's a feature built into the phone line itself, made for an honest purpose: sending your calls to a number you chose when you can't answer. You'll find it in the settings of the app you dial from, and sometimes under network settings.

Once it's on, the network hands the incoming call to the forwarded number **before** your phone ever rings. So it isn't a missed call. The call never arrived.

One detail flips the whole diagnosis: **forwarding only affects incoming calls.** Outgoing calls keep working normally — the internet, social media, banking, all fine. So any attempt to reassure yourself by testing the line itself ("I can dial out, the line works") will reassure you falsely.

## The method, step by step

**1. A call shaped like a service.** "This is the telecom company," or "the bank," or "customer support for something." The identity changes; the content doesn't: "there's an action to complete, type this code on the screen."

**2. What they type isn't a code they received.** This is where it turns. What gets typed into a dialler isn't a verification code — it's an instruction that executes against the line, and it enables call forwarding to a number the caller dictated. The sequence starts with asterisks and digits, of the shape `*21*` or `**21*`. Those are examples of the **shape** only. They are not instructions, and nobody should type them — not you, not them. There is no legitimate reason for anyone to type something like that into a dialler screen.

**3. Nothing rings on their end.** From that moment, whoever calls her — her children, a friend, the bank — is answered by someone else. And there is no strange sound in her ear to stop her.

**4. The real verification code goes to the other number.** Apps that send a verification code — WhatsApp, email, the banking app — send that call or message to the number on file. That number now reaches him. Every "security step" we've spent years insisting on becomes the key.

**5. "Forgot password."** This is the second move, and it's the one that takes the account rather than just the calls. Someone requests a password reset, the system sends verification to the number on file, and that number is his to read. New password, and he's in.

**6. Once he's in, he widens it.** Devices linked to WhatsApp, the email that every other account resets through, the banking app. Anything you could have cancelled with a phone call now becomes a race.

## Why nothing we teach each other works here

| The advice we repeat | What actually happens |
|---|---|
| "Never give anyone your code" | Nobody asked her for a code. She typed something into a dialler believing she was completing a procedure. |
| "Never tell anyone your password" | The password was never spoken. It was reset from the inside. |
| "Don't trust calls from the bank" | The call came, the request was made, and the bank dialled the other number. There was no moment where anything stopped. |
| "You'd notice if something broke" | Nothing is broken. Incoming was diverted; outgoing still works. |

The reason is that this method is built to pass over all of those habits, because it **asks for nothing**. And it targets older people specifically, because they trust speech, and they treat anyone talking to them politely as someone providing a service. Nobody here is stupid. The design is.

## The check: two minutes, three things

This check is done by you, in your hand — not over the phone, describing where to tap. You do it in front of them, on the phone in front of you.

**1. Open the setting and look at it yourself.** The app they dial from → its settings → call forwarding. If there's a number there nobody recognises, cancel it from that same screen immediately.

**2. Ask one question.** "Has anyone called you recently and asked you to type a code or a number into your phone?" If the answer is yes, that's the alarm — even if the call ended politely and everything seemed fine. The thing people don't remember is the thing that produced no sensation.

**3. Call the carrier from a different phone.** Not from theirs. Reach the official number written somewhere obvious, and ask for confirmation that call forwarding is off on the line.

**Why don't I just give you the cancel code?** Because the deactivation code differs by carrier and by line, and any sequence I pin down here is a guess that can waste your time or land you in the same hole. The only party that can confirm it is your carrier, or the setting above. Better than both: open the setting with your own eyes instead of relying on what someone told you.

## If you find a number that isn't theirs: recovery, in order

**First, close the door.** Call the carrier from another phone and cancel call forwarding. Anything you do before this step is being done on a line that isn't yours.

**Second, recover the master key.** Change the password on the email address those accounts reset through. Do it from your own device, inside the app or the real site — not from a link that arrived in a message. Turn on two-step verification for it.

**Third, evict whoever's inside.** Re-secure WhatsApp and the banking app **from inside the app itself**. On WhatsApp: Settings → Linked Devices → log out every device that isn't yours, and enable two-step verification. Unauthorised access registers as a linked device, and that device keeps working even after you change the password.

**Fourth, if there's a banking app:** call the bank's hotline from the card or the official site **before** you do anything else. This is not the place for blame, and the separation is real — every minute after you find out is more room inside the account.

**Fifth, don't run the recovery from the same line.** Every step above has to happen from another phone, or at home on the internet — not from the line that carries the forwarding.

## Three sentences, not ten

Ten sentences don't get remembered, and a person who feels tested doesn't answer. Three sentences, said exactly like this:

**Any call that asks you to type a number or code while you're on the phone = don't type it, hang up, tell me.**

**Anyone who says they're from the telecom company or the bank and wants you to do something on screen = hang up, and call them back from the official number written somewhere obvious.**

**If you feel something happened, even something small, tell your children straight away. There is no such thing as embarrassment here.**

The third is the most important of the three. Not because the incident happens, but because what happens is decided in the first ten minutes: the person who says it immediately gets it back, and the person who waits spends the time that would have been enough.

## This is not their fault

If this has already happened, the first sentence out of your mouth is: "this isn't your fault, the trick was very clever." That isn't comfort — it's operational. Because if you make them feel stupid, next time they won't tell you. And silence is what keeps this method working: they aren't afraid of phone calls, they're afraid of being embarrassed in front of their kids.

The idea that an older relative could fall for this isn't evidence of weakness. It's evidence that the trap doesn't show itself. Every one of us has taken our father's or mother's phone to fix something, and typed into the screen while completely at ease. The only difference this time is that the person who typed it was working against the line.

Go look at your parents' phones today. Open the setting in front of you, ask the one question, and call the carrier from another phone. Do the three things with your own hands — don't just forward this article.
